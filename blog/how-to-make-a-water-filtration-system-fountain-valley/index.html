How to Make a Water Filtration System: What Fountain Valley, CA Homes Need to Know

A home water filtration system is a chain of stages, each aimed at a specific problem. Before you buy parts, you need to know what is in your water and what you want to change.

This guide explains the stages, the limits of filters and how Fountain Valley's hard water changes the plan.

By the Fountain Valley Pro Plumbing team · Updated November 2026

Start with what you want to fix

People install filters for different reasons. Some want better taste or less chlorine smell. Some want a specific contaminant reduced. Others are fighting scale on fixtures. Each goal needs a different tool, and no single filter does everything.

Write down your goal first. Then check your water provider's annual report to see what is actually in the supply.

What the city report says

The City of Fountain Valley's 2026 water quality report, covering 2025 data, lists average hardness of 217 milligrams per liter, or 13 grains per gallon, with a range of 10 to 15. The report also states that the city supplied groundwater only in 2025.

Hardness is a mineral issue, and carbon or sediment filters do not remove it. If scale is your concern, you need a softener or a conditioner. If taste is your concern, carbon helps. Read the full report on the city's website before choosing.

Source type: City of Fountain Valley 2026 Water Quality Report

Whole-house or point-of-use

A whole-house system treats all water as it enters. It protects fixtures and appliances, but it needs space, a bypass and professional plumbing. A point-of-use system, such as an under-sink reverse osmosis unit, treats one tap, usually for drinking.

Many homes use both. A sediment and carbon filter at the main plus an under-sink unit at the kitchen is a common pattern.

Planning an install

1

Use the city report, a hardness test kit and, if needed, a lab test.

2

Pick stages that match your goals, and avoid paying for unneeded treatment.

3

Find the location

A whole-house unit needs space near the main with a drain and power if required.

4

Install with bypass valves

Valves let you service the unit without shutting off the house water.

Be cautious with claims

Marketing for filters often implies broad health benefits. Look for third-party certification for the specific contaminant you care about. If a safety question is involved, ask your water provider or a lab.

Maintenance matters

Filters clog, carbon saturates and softener resin ages. Replace cartridges on schedule, or flow and quality drop. Keep a log of install dates and change dates.

A neglected filter can become a source of bacterial growth. If a filter has sat unused, flush it as the manufacturer directs before drinking from it.

Choosing between cartridge and tank systems

Cartridge filters are compact housings that hold a replaceable filter. They are simple and inexpensive, and they suit sediment and carbon stages. Tank systems hold media such as carbon or resin in a large vessel. They handle higher flow and last longer between changes, but they take more room.

Match the system to your flow. A whole-house setup that is too small drops pressure, and one that is too large wastes money. A plumber can size it from the number of fixtures and the pipe diameter.

Drinking water versus fixture protection

Two goals often get mixed. If you want better drinking water, treat the kitchen tap with carbon or reverse osmosis. If you want to protect fixtures and appliances from scale, treat the whole house with a softener or conditioner. They are separate problems with separate tools.

Most households do well with one of each. A small kitchen unit handles taste, and a whole-house unit handles hardness. Skipping the second stage means scale keeps building in the water heater.

Questions to ask a seller

Ask what the system removes and what third-party certification supports the claim. Ask what upkeep it needs, what each filter costs and how often you replace it. Ask where the waste water goes and whether the drain connection is allowed in your area.

A seller who cannot answer those questions plainly is not worth hiring. Avoid anyone who promises to fix every water problem with one product.

Standards to look for on a filter

Look for third-party certification to NSF/ANSI standards for the contaminant you care about, such as NSF/ANSI 42 for taste and odor and NSF/ANSI 53 for health-related contaminants. Plumbing connections follow the California Plumbing Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).
