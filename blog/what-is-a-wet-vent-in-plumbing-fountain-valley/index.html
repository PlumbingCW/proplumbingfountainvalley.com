What Is a Wet Vent in Plumbing? What Fountain Valley, CA Homes Need to Know

A wet vent is a single pipe that does two jobs. It carries waste from one fixture and also vents another. When designed correctly it saves pipe and space, but a wrong layout causes slow drains and gurgling.

This guide explains the idea in plain language, shows where remodels go wrong and points Fountain Valley homeowners to the city's permit contact.

By the Fountain Valley Pro Plumbing team · Updated November 2026

Venting, in one paragraph

Every fixture trap needs air. Without it, water flowing down a drain creates suction that can pull water out of nearby traps and let sewer gas in. Plumbers solve this with vent pipes. In the simplest layout each fixture has its own vent that runs up to the roof.

That is a lot of pipe. A wet vent shortens it by letting one section of drain pipe also serve as a vent for a second fixture, as long as the pipe is sized correctly and the layout follows the plumbing code.

How a wet vent works

In a typical bathroom group, a sink drain connects to a pipe that rises to a vent. A toilet or tub drain may connect into that same pipe lower down. Air from the vent flows through the same pipe that carries the sink's waste. The pipe is larger than a plain drain, which leaves room for air above the water.

The key is that the vented pipe stays wide enough and that fixtures connect in a permitted order. When a plumber violates these, the vent can fill with water and stop working.

Wet vent vs a dedicated vent

Both are legal when done correctly. They suit different layouts.

Problems may only show when several fixtures run at once.

A trap siphoned dry because of poor venting.

Water is rushing in a pipe that cannot vent.

Who sets the rules in Fountain Valley

Plumbing layout rules come from the plumbing code adopted by the state and enforced locally. In Fountain Valley, the Building Division at 10200 Slater Avenue handles permits and inspections. You can reach it at 714-593-4429, and the Permit Center portal accepts applications electronically.

Venting changes are among the things inspectors check. A bathroom remodel that moves fixtures usually needs a permit, and an inspection happens before walls are closed. Ask the Building Division what applies, since rules vary by project.

Source type: City of Fountain Valley Building Division

Why remodels go wrong

Moving a bathroom sink, adding a second toilet or opening a wall to relocate a tub can change how air reaches the traps. A layout that worked in the original build may not work after the change. Contractors who do not do plumbing every day sometimes guess.

If you plan to move fixtures, ask a plumber to review the drain and vent layout before the walls are closed. Fixing venting after drywall is up is expensive.

Questions to ask your contractor

Ask how the new fixtures will be vented, whether a wet vent is part of the plan and who pulls the permit. Ask for the plumbing inspection to be scheduled before drywall.

If you already have a venting problem

A plumber can test by running several fixtures at once and listening for gurgling. A camera or smoke test can find blocked or disconnected vents. Sometimes the fix is a small addition such as an air admittance valve, if the code allows it in your location.

Slow drains from clogs can look like vent problems. Start with drain cleaning to rule out a blockage, and then look at the vent system.

Reading your own bathroom

You can often see parts of the system. Look in the cabinet under the sink for the drain pipe and note where it goes into the wall. In a basement or crawlspace, you can see horizontal drains and vertical stacks. If you cannot find a vent for a fixture, a wet vent may be serving it.

That does not mean something is wrong. It means the layout is compact. Only a plumber can judge whether it meets code.

Where wet vents come from in the code

Wet venting is allowed under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), when pipe sizes and fixture groupings match the code tables. A layout that skips those limits is the kind inspectors reject, so ask for the inspection before drywall goes up.
