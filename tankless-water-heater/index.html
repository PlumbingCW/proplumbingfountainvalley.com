How tankless heaters work

A tankless heater turns on when a tap opens. A flow sensor starts the burner or elements and a heat exchanger warms the water as it passes through. There is no stored supply to run out, which is why households with big demand like them.

The trade-offs are cost, gas capacity and maintenance. A tankless unit may need a larger gas line than a tank heater, and a vent that suits the exhaust. It also needs descaling in hard water areas.

Symptoms that call for a service visit

Most tankless problems fall into a few categories.

Error codes

Often scale, a flow problem or a venting issue. The code points to the right test.

Cold water sandwich

A brief cold burst between hot water uses, common with short draws.

Weak hot water flow

Scale in the heat exchanger or a clogged inlet filter.

Unit shuts off mid-use

Overheating from scale or a flame sensor fault.

Descaling and hard water

Fountain Valley's 2026 water quality report lists average hardness of 13 grains per gallon with a range of 10 to 15. Minerals bake onto a tankless heat exchanger, narrow its passages and trigger error codes. A flush with a descaling solution removes the scale.

Manufacturers commonly recommend a yearly flush in hard water areas. A water softener reduces how fast scale forms. The plumber can tell you whether a flush or a softener is the better first step.

Installing a tankless unit

A tankless install often needs a gas line check, a new vent, a condensate drain for high-efficiency models and sometimes an electrical outlet. The unit mounts on a wall. The plumber confirms these before work begins.

If you are switching from a tank, tell the plumber about your peak demand. Two showers and a dishwasher running together is the usual test.

Questions to ask before you buy

Ask what the gas line must carry, whether venting needs to change, where the condensate will drain and who handles the permit. Ask what the maintenance schedule looks like and what a flush costs.

Also ask about the warranty. Some require proof of yearly maintenance. A plumber who installs units regularly will know the details.

Standards for gas and venting

Tankless installs follow the California Plumbing Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), along with the manufacturer's venting and gas sizing rules. Electric units follow the California Electrical Code, based on the National Electrical Code (NEC). Skipping a vent or gas check is how safety problems start.

Is tankless right for you?

Tankless suits households that value endless hot water, have space limits or want a smaller footprint. It is less appealing if your gas supply is limited, your hot-water use is light or you dislike regular maintenance.

The plumber will help you weigh these. A tank heater is still a sound choice for many homes.
